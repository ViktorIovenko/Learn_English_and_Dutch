How the parallel learning method works

One word opens the door to several languages at once — the mechanics.

ParallelLingvo Team
·Sep 11, 2026·6 min read

Most vocabulary apps repeat the same idea for every language pair: one deck for Spanish, another for German, a third for Dutch — three separate lists of the same underlying words. ParallelLingvo starts from a different assumption. A word isn't three unrelated facts to memorize three times; it's one concept that happens to have several names. So instead of duplicating decks, every card holds one concept with all of its translations attached to it at once.

One card, every language you're learning

When you open a lesson, each card shows your word's translation in every language you've currently selected — not just the one pair you'd normally be studying. Add a third or fourth language later, and it slots into the same cards retroactively; you don't rebuild anything.

One of those languages is always the "target" for that pass through the card: its translation stays hidden, and you spell it out yourself, letter by letter, instead of just picking it out of a multiple-choice list. Recognizing a word and being able to produce it are different skills, and only one of them is useful in a real conversation.

Translations stay attached to the same concept, so a session in one lesson moves every selected language forward.

Why typing beats tapping

Flashcard apps that only ask you to recognize a word train recognition — useful for reading, not for speaking or writing. Typing forces active recall: your brain has to reconstruct the word instead of just confirming it looks familiar. Get a letter wrong and the card shows you exactly where the mistake happened, so the correction is specific instead of a vague "try again."

Every correct answer also unlocks pronunciation audio for that word, generated per language, so you leave each card having seen it, spelled it, and heard it.

None of this requires you to slow down and study one language at a time. The parallel structure means a single study session — the same words, the same ten minutes — moves every language you're learning forward together, instead of splitting your time between separate apps or separate decks for each one.
